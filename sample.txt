The old lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral stairs every evening at dusk. He counted the steps out of habit, one hundred and twelve, though he had known the number for thirty years. At the top he wiped the salt from the glass, trimmed the wick, and watched the first beam sweep across the dark water.

The village below depended on the light more than anyone admitted. Fishing boats returned late in winter, when fog rolled in from the open sea and swallowed the harbor wall. Without the beam, the rocks at the mouth of the bay would have claimed a boat every season.

In the spring a young engineer arrived with plans to replace the lamp with an automatic system. The keeper listened politely, poured him tea, and showed him the logbook where every storm of the last three decades was recorded in careful handwriting.

"The machine will not get tired," the engineer said. "It will not get sick, and it will not forget." The keeper nodded. "That is true," he answered. "But it will not notice when the sea changes its mind."

They spent the summer working side by side. The engineer installed sensors and cables; the keeper taught him to read the color of the clouds and the smell of the wind before a gale. By autumn the new system was running, and the old man still climbed the stairs each night, simply to watch.

Bread is one of the oldest prepared foods. Flour, water, salt, and yeast are mixed into a dough, which is kneaded to develop gluten. The gluten forms a stretchy network that traps the gas produced by the yeast, allowing the dough to rise.

After the first rise, the dough is shaped and left to rise again. Baking at a high temperature sets the structure, browns the crust, and drives off much of the moisture. A loaf is done when it sounds hollow when tapped on the bottom.

Sourdough replaces commercial yeast with a starter, a culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria. The bacteria give the bread its tangy flavor, and the slower fermentation often makes the loaf easier to digest.

A river begins as small streams high in the mountains, fed by rain and melting snow. As the streams join, the water gathers speed and carves valleys into the rock. Over thousands of years the river shapes the land around it as much as the land shapes the river.

Near the sea the current slows, and the river drops the sand and silt it has carried for hundreds of kilometers. These deposits build wide, flat deltas, which are often among the most fertile farmland in the world.
